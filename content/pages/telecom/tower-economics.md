## What a tower actually is

A cell tower is a physical structure, a mast, a rooftop installation, or a monopole, that holds the antennas and radio equipment forming one "cell" in a mobile network. Each tower covers a limited geographic area; the network is the sum of thousands or millions of overlapping cells handing signal off to each other as a device moves. Coverage gaps, dropped calls, and slow data usually trace back to a simple physical reality: not enough towers, or not enough capacity on the towers that exist, in that specific location.

## The equipment stack on a tower

A typical tower carries **antennas** (the visible panels that send and receive signal), **radio units** (which convert signal between the antenna and the network), and often a **small equipment shelter** at the base housing power systems, backup batteries or generators, and the connection back to the core network, usually fiber, though microwave links are still common in harder-to-reach locations. Multiple operators frequently share the same tower, each with their own equipment, which is why a single tower structure often carries several sets of antennas stacked at different heights.

## Why towers became a separate business

For decades, operators owned their own towers outright. Starting in the 2000s and accelerating since, most large operators globally have sold off their tower portfolios to specialized **tower companies (TowerCos)**, then leased that same infrastructure back. This wasn't a sign of financial distress, it was a deliberate capital strategy: towers are a slow, steady, real-estate-like asset, while spectrum, network technology, and subscriber relationships are where an operator's actual competitive advantage lives. Selling towers freed up capital for operators to spend on those higher-return priorities, while TowerCos, who specialize purely in maximizing infrastructure returns, could run the physical asset more efficiently.

## How TowerCos make money

A TowerCo's core business is leasing space on a tower to multiple operators simultaneously, tenants, in industry terms. The financial engine of the tower business is the **tenancy ratio**: how many operators share a single tower structure. A tower with one tenant barely covers its own maintenance cost. A tower with three or four tenants, however, sees revenue multiply while the underlying fixed cost of the structure barely changes, since it's the same steel and land either way. This is why TowerCos aggressively pursue co-location deals, and why tenancy ratio is the single most important efficiency metric in that part of the industry.

## Where towers are heading

Two trends are reshaping tower economics right now. First, **5G densification**: because 5G's higher-frequency spectrum travels shorter distances than older technology, operators need meaningfully more cell sites, particularly smaller, lower-power ones, in dense urban areas to deliver full coverage. This is expanding demand for "small cell" infrastructure, a different physical form factor from a traditional macro tower, often mounted on lampposts or building sides.

Second, **edge computing** is starting to turn tower sites into more than just radio infrastructure. Placing compute capacity physically at or near a tower site can reduce latency for applications that need it, autonomous systems, AR/VR, industrial automation, which is opening a new potential revenue line for tower operators beyond simply renting antenna space.
