## The basic shape of the business

A telecom operator sells access to a network it spent years and billions building. That single fact explains almost everything about how the industry behaves. Unlike a software company, where the cost of serving one more customer is close to zero, a telecom operator's costs are dominated by fixed infrastructure: spectrum licenses, towers, fiber, and core network equipment that has to be paid for whether one subscriber uses it or a million do.

This creates a business with unusually high operating leverage. Once the network is built, each additional subscriber costs relatively little to serve, so revenue growth flows disproportionately to profit. It also means operators are financially exposed for years before a network generation (3G, 4G, 5G) pays for itself, which is why telecom is one of the most capital-intensive industries in the world.

## Where the revenue actually comes from

**Consumer mobile** is still the largest single revenue line for most operators: monthly plans, prepaid top-ups, and increasingly, bundled data packages. **Fixed broadband** (fiber, cable, or fixed wireless) is the second major pillar, especially as home data consumption keeps climbing.

**Enterprise and B2B services** are the fastest-growing segment for most large operators. This covers private networks, IoT connectivity, managed security, and increasingly, cloud and edge computing services sold alongside connectivity. Enterprise deals carry lower volume but often much higher margin and stickiness than consumer plans, since switching an entire company's network provider is a far bigger decision than switching a personal phone plan.

**Wholesale revenue** comes from other operators or MVNOs paying to use the network, and from tower or infrastructure leasing where an operator hasn't fully divested those assets.

## Why churn and lifetime value dominate strategy

Because acquiring a new subscriber is expensive (marketing, subsidized devices, retail infrastructure) and each subscriber only becomes profitable after months of retained revenue, the entire commercial strategy of a telecom operator is built around **customer lifetime value versus acquisition cost**. This is why so much operator behavior, aggressive retention offers, long contract terms, bundling, loyalty programs, only makes sense once you see churn as the single biggest lever on profitability, more so than pricing.

## The capital cycle

Telecom runs on a repeating cycle: raise capital, build network capacity ahead of demand, monetize that capacity over several years, then repeat for the next network generation. This is why operators carry significant debt as a normal part of their capital structure, and why a spike in capital expenditure (like a 5G rollout) is read by investors as an investment in future capacity, not a sign of distress, as long as it eventually converts into revenue growth.

## What makes telecom different from a typical tech business

A tech platform can scale globally with a relatively thin local footprint. A telecom operator generally cannot: physical infrastructure, spectrum licenses, and regulation are tied to specific countries or regions. This is why the telecom industry looks more like a collection of national or regional champions than a handful of global winners, and why most telecom M&A activity happens within a single country's borders rather than across them.
