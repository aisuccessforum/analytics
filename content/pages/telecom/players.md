## Operators (Telcos)

Companies that hold spectrum licenses and sell connectivity directly to consumers and businesses — the brand names most people recognize. Their competitive position is shaped by spectrum holdings, network quality, and bundling strategy.

## Equipment vendors

Companies that build the RAN and core network hardware and software operators buy to run their networks. This is a concentrated market — a small number of vendors supply most of the world's network infrastructure, which makes vendor selection a geopolitical as well as commercial decision in many markets.

## Tower companies (TowerCos)

Own and lease physical tower infrastructure to multiple operators. Their business model is closer to real estate than telecom — steady, contracted, infrastructure-style returns rather than the more cyclical economics of running a network.

## MVNOs

Resell network capacity under their own brand without owning infrastructure. Common in markets with strong wholesale regulation; their competitive edge is usually a niche audience or a low-cost, no-frills positioning the host operator doesn't want to pursue itself under its main brand.

## Regulators

Set spectrum policy, license terms, and — in most markets — retail pricing guardrails for essential services. An operator's strategy cannot be fully understood without accounting for the regulatory environment it operates in, since spectrum allocation and merger approval sit entirely in regulators' hands.

## Hyperscalers and cloud providers

Increasingly relevant as operators move core network functions onto cloud infrastructure and as edge computing creates new revenue-sharing arrangements between telecom and cloud players.
